Why now · 1 of 2
We already
knew how

Requirements engineering, UML, BPMN — software engineering taught all of it. Then we mostly dropped it. Heavy upfront modeling went stale the moment the code moved, locked in decisions before we'd learned anything, and cost too much to maintain. Agile won, for good reasons.

Why now · 2 of 2
Those reasons
just expired

LLMs collapse the cost of producing requirements — and, crucially, of regenerating them. That kills the staleness objection: the spec becomes a living document, re-derived as you learn, not a frozen waterfall artifact. And agents need intent made explicit more than human teams ever did — they can't fill the gaps with tacit knowledge.

Cheap to write Cheap to keep true Iterative, not waterfall
The one idea
The spec is the
source of truth

Flip the order. You write down what you want before any code exists, and that written spec — not the code — becomes the contract. The code is just an artifact: it should be safe to delete and regenerate from the spec.

Why it works
Shrink the
realm of possibles

An agent doesn't retrieve your solution — it samples from a space of plausible ones. Every unstated constraint is a degree of freedom it fills from its training prior, not your intent. A spec collapses that space until the model's most likely output lands on your bullseye. Requirements engineering is prompt engineering at project scale — persistent, shared, reviewable.

Fewer degrees of freedom Constrain where it can't read your mind Leave slack where it beats you

In the wild · one tool that does this
Spec-Kit

GitHub's open-source toolkit. It gives you a structured spec → plan → tasks process that bolts onto the agent you already use — Claude Code, Copilot, Gemini CLI. You describe intent; it scaffolds the spec, the plan, and the task breakdown the agent then builds against.

github.com/github/spec-kit ↗
In the wild · another that does this
BMAD-Method

"Breakthrough Method for Agile AI-Driven Development." It goes further, modelling a whole dev team as specialized agent personas — analyst, PM, architect, dev — that produce and hand off the spec, the architecture, and the stories between them. Same goal, more of the process made explicit.

github.com/bmad-code-org/BMAD-METHOD ↗
Where it really pays off
The PR
nobody can review

You've seen it: a vibecoded pull request, 2,000 lines, and no one — not even the author — can say what it does or why it's built that way. Vibecoding tends to sprawl. With a spec, the reviewer reads the intent and the architecture first; the diff traces back to a contract, and the PR can be scoped to it instead of ballooning.

Intent before diff Architecture is stated, not guessed Smaller, scoped PRs

What you get
Why bother
  • Intent is reviewable — people approve the spec, not just the diff.
  • The spec outlives the code — regenerate without losing the why.
  • Parallel agents stay aligned to one shared contract.
  • Onboarding and handoff get a document, not a chat log.
The honest take
It isn't free

Writing specs is overhead. For a throwaway script or a five-minute fix, the ceremony costs more than it saves — that's still vibecoding territory. Spec-driven earns its keep when the work is real, shared, or long-lived. Match the weight to the stakes.
